\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

We erased lesions from medical images and made a matched control edit elsewhere in each image; nine comparison models, correct on $82.9\%$ to $88.8\%$ of lesion images, still reported the finding on $41.1\%$ to $61.6\%$ of 219 erased images. \ms, trained on such images, reported it on $9.6\%$, but on the fresh external set it was correct on fewer lesion images than \gem{} ($47.6\%$ against $74.1\%$), and its advantage did not carry over to unedited colonoscopy pairs (natural ESG $0.243$, as for its base model). \msn, a post hoc model trained also on unedited images, had a natural ESG of $0.612$ $\ci{0.526}{0.698}$ at a hospital not used in training, above two of the three reference models; a difference from \gem{} ($0.086$ $\ci{-0.017}{0.190}$) was not shown, and on the fresh external set it reported more erased lesions than \ms{} ($22.0\%$ against $8.3\%$). On the 20 of 36 erasures that a radiologist or a gastroenterologist accepted as free of the finding, the comparison models still reported it on $43.3\%$ $\ci{31.7}{56.1}$ and \ms{} on 1 of 20.
% src: 04_results_a.tex subsec:res_main (82.9-88.8%; 41.1-61.6% of 219), subsec:res_trained (9.6%), subsec:res_external (47.6% against 74.1%) and subsec:res_readers (20 of 36 accepted; 43.3% [31.7, 56.1]; 1 of 20); 05_results_b.tex subsec:res_pairs (0.243 for both; 0.612 [0.526, 0.698]; 0.086 [-0.017, 0.190]) and subsec:res_nmodel (22.0% against 8.3%)

These results do not show that the comparison models would report a lesion on half of the lesion-free images met in practice; they show that removing the lesion region often left the answer unchanged. On unedited lesion-free images the same models reported a finding on $9.0\%$ to $30.9\%$, and on a blank image with the same question on none, so the question alone did not produce the reports. What in the edited image led to them (the organ, the instrument, the surrounding tissue or residual lesion) was not identified, and some may be correct readings of residual evidence: the specialists called the finding present on 16 of 36 erasures. The persistence, however, did not depend on such images; it held on the erasures that the specialists accepted. ESG therefore measures how far an answer depends on the lesion region, not error against a clinical reference standard. The persistence was seen in each of the nine comparison models and, for the three reference models, in every imaging type and under every prompting condition tested. Accuracy on unaltered images would not have revealed it: models that differed by less than 6 percentage points in lesion images correct differed by about 20 percentage points in erased-lesion claims.
% src: 04_results_a.tex subsec:res_main (9.0-30.9%; blank image: none of the nine models, L1784-1805; every model at least 41.1%; reference models at least 31% in every imaging type, L1630-1641, and 36.8-66.2% under six prompting conditions, L1680-1681; "less than 6" and "about 20")

Detection of the edit is the main alternative explanation for the result of \ms, which was trained on images built like those of the test. A model that answers ``absent'' whenever an image shows traces of editing does so on the control edit too, and its ESG is zero. The answers also followed the amount of lesion removed, from $4.2\%$ denied after a distant control edit to $86.7\%$ when the whole mask was erased (Section~\ref{subsec:res_alternatives}). The advantage of \ms{} persisted, at a lower ESG, with editing tools not used in training and on erasures that an edit detector scored as unedited, with some sensitivity to unfamiliar edit traces (Section~\ref{subsec:res_alternatives}). The unedited pairs do not settle the question for \ms, whose fewer reports of a polyp on polyp-free frames than its base model were offset by more requests for a better image (Section~\ref{subsec:res_pairs}). The data fit best answers that follow mainly whether the lesion region had been erased, with a contribution from edit traces that these tests cannot exclude.
% src: 04_results_a.tex subsec:res_alternatives (LaMa, 143 validation lesions: 4.2%, 86.7% = v5 P1305, L1269-1271; tools not used in training; low-visibility erasures; closing sentence); 05_results_b.tex subsec:res_pairs (2 against 15 polyp-free frames with a polyp reported; 12 more requests for a better image)

Training on counterfactual views changed the answers on edited images and did not improve detection on unedited ones. It raised the main-test ESG of first-stage models from $0.279$ to $0.741$ (means over three seeds), and on a second base model from $0.374$ to $0.721$. On unedited colonoscopy pairs it raised the share of polyp-free frames answered ``absent'' or referred and lowered the share of polyp frames found, and once unedited pairs were in training it was not shown to add anything there (Section~\ref{subsec:res_pairs}). Detection on unedited images changed with unedited training images: \msn{} answered ``absent'' or referred on $99.1\%$ of polyp-free frames at the hospital not used in training (reference models $75.0\%$ to $83.6\%$), and its Youden index for pneumothorax on unedited films exceeded that of each reference model. Changes of the training objective, the adapter or the agent design were not shown to do better: none of seventeen alternative designs and none of 270 decision rules met its criterion (Section~\ref{subsec:res_designs}). One explanation, not tested here, is that an erased view shows a familiar context without its lesion, which may discourage answers made from context while adding no example of a lesion.

These findings agree with earlier evidence that accuracy does not establish image use. \citet{jin2024hidden} found by physician reading that $35.5\%$ of the correct answers of one model came with a flawed rationale, most often in image comprehension ($27.2\%$). Accuracy fell from $84\%$ to $28\%$ for one model when a vignette misled \citep{buckley2026text}. Four of five models answered $33.7\%$ to $37.7\%$ of 175 image-dependent questions correctly without the image, against $20\%$ by chance \citep{gu2025illusion}, and four systems kept about half of their correct answers when the radiograph was exchanged for one with the opposite label \citep{lotfinia2026image}. Our results differ in one respect: given a blank image, every comparison model asked for a better image, so the reports did not survive removal of the image. The difference may lie in the design, as our questions carry no clinical vignette and our protocol allows such a request. Removing or exchanging the whole image cannot detect reports that arise only when an image of the right kind is shown.

Erasure with a matched control can establish whether an answer depends on what the lesion region shows, across models, images and editing tools. It cannot establish that the finding is absent for a specialist, because secondary signs outside the mask, such as the mass effect of a brain tumour or the posterior acoustic shadowing of a breast mass, may remain. It also suits only findings that a mask can enclose. A diffuse finding may survive erasure (on QaTa-COV19 the ESG of \ms{} was $0.05$), and a pneumothorax, recognised by a pleural line and absent lung markings beyond it, may not be removed by filling its mask. Unedited same-patient pairs avoid these problems but exist only where a dataset marks the finding frame by frame.
% src: L1687 (external datasets by source: MedScope-4B ESG on QaTa-COV19 0.05)

This study has five main limitations. (i) Not every erasure was accepted by readers: the specialists called the finding present on 16 of 36 LaMa erasures and both trainees on 13 of 32, so the main-test rates include images with residual evidence; on the 16 erasures that a specialist called present, \ms{} reported the finding on 3, so part of its low rate may be denial of residual evidence. The specialist analysis rests on one reader per imaging type and 20 accepted erasures; further radiologists are reading. Two of the four readers who have finished are co-authors; all read without labels. The lesion-free labels are imperfect too: the trainees called a finding present on 23 of 64 reads of lesion-free images, and on 26 of the 288 lesion-free images at least seven of the nine comparison models reported a finding. In colonoscopy, REAL-Colon marks only resected polyps, and an LDPolypVideo polyp-free frame may lie as few as 5 frames from a polyp frame, so some polyp-free frames may show a polyp; the comparisons there were unchanged with pairs at least 50 frames apart. ESG compares two edits of one image and does not use these labels. (ii) Confirmation on unedited images covers colonoscopy frames and pneumothorax films, with three of the nine comparison models; \texttt{claude-sonnet-5-5}, whose main-test ESG was higher than that of \opus, was not run on them. \msn{} was not shown to exceed \gem{} on colonoscopy pairs (lower limit of the difference at LDPolypVideo $-0.017$), its pneumothorax films came from a source used in its training, and its detection on new SIIM-ACR patients varied between seeds (54, 75 and 70 of 100). (iii) The main test had been used with development versions of the trained model. On the fresh external set, run with no model before \ms{} was fixed, it reported $8.3\%$ of erased lesions but was correct on $47.6\%$ of lesion images, against $74.1\%$ for \gem. (iv) The tests were yes/no questions on single images of focal lesions from public datasets, to which the comparison models may have been exposed. Lesion-free images were not always from the anatomical sites of the lesion images, which affects lesion-free claims but not ESG. Each comparison model was run once, no task-specific detector was included, and the open-ended test (36 cases) did not confirm the advantage; all nine comparison models showed the persistence, which bounds the effect of a single run. (v) \msn{} was designed after earlier results were known, although its tests were fixed before it was trained, and the analysis plan is our own dated record. Net utility rests on a cost matrix that we wrote and used as the training reward, so it favours the trained model; ESG and the claim rates do not depend on it. No model was evaluated prospectively.

Readings by further radiologists will show whether the result on accepted erasures holds with more than one reader per imaging type. Unedited same-patient pairs with a verified reference standard are needed beyond colonoscopy; the chest X-ray follow-up pairs used here were a post hoc secondary analysis. The consequence for patients of a reported finding that the image does not support can be measured only prospectively, in clinical use.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

On public images and yes/no questions, nine comparison models reported the finding on $41.1\%$ to $61.6\%$ of images from which the lesion region had been erased, and on no blank image. A small open-weight model trained on matched counterfactual views reported it on $9.6\%$ while correct on $84.9\%$ of lesion images, which shows that the dependence of an answer on the lesion region can be measured and changed by training. The persistence held on erasures that specialists accepted as free of the finding. The change did not improve detection on unedited images; a post hoc model trained also on unedited images did improve there, without being shown to exceed the best reference model. More readers per imaging type and unedited pairs beyond colonoscopy are needed before the measure can be read clinically.
