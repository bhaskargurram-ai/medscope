\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

We erased lesions from medical images, made a matched control edit elsewhere in each image, and asked the same closed question of every view. Nine comparison models were correct on $0.829$ to $0.888$ of lesion images and still reported the finding on $41.1\%$ to $61.6\%$ of 219 erased images. \ms, trained on such views, reported it on $9.6\%$ (ESG $0.758$ \ci{0.701}{0.815} against $0.269$ to $0.470$). On unedited colonoscopy frame pairs \ms{} had the natural ESG of its base model ($0.243$), and \msn, trained in addition on unedited images, reached $0.612$ \ci{0.526}{0.698} at a hospital not used in training. That was above \ms{} and two of the three reference models; the difference from \gem{} ($0.086$ \ci{-0.017}{0.190}) was not shown. \msn{} also reported more erased lesions than \msp{} on the fresh external set ($22.0\%$ against $11.3\%$).
% src: 04_results_a.tex subsec:res_main (0.829-0.888; 41.1-61.6% of 219; 9.6%; 0.758 [0.701, 0.815]; 0.269-0.470); 05_results_b.tex subsec:res_pairs (0.243; 0.612 [0.526, 0.698]; +0.086 [-0.017, +0.190]) and subsec:res_nmodel (22.0% against 11.3%)

These results do not show that the comparison models would report a lesion on half of the lesion-free images met in practice; they show that removing the lesion often left the answer unchanged. Erased images do not occur in clinical use, and on unedited lesion-free images the same models reported a finding on $9.0\%$ to $30.9\%$. The share of erased lesions reported is therefore not a clinical error rate. Nor did the question alone produce the reports. Shown a blank image with the same question, no model reported the finding in any of 183 cases, and at most $2.1\%$ of 141 images of another imaging type drew such a report (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:s_blank}). The models reported findings that the rest of the image may suggest but no longer shows. What suggests them (the organ, the instrument, the surrounding tissue or an incomplete erasure) was not identified.
% src: 04_results_a.tex subsec:res_main (lesion-free claims 9.0-30.9%; blank-image control: 0% of 183 cases for every model); L1786-1793 (image of another imaging type, 141 of 183 cases: finding reported on 0 to 2.1% by the nine comparison models, 2.1% by MedScope-4B and by Qwen3.5-4B)

Detection of the edit is the main alternative explanation for the result of \ms, which was trained on views built like those of the test. Three results argue against it. An answerer that responds only to the edit has an ESG of zero by construction, and \ms{} denied $14.6\%$ of control-edited lesions against $90.4\%$ of erased ones. Its lead persisted, at a lower ESG, with two editing tools unseen in training and on erasures that an edit detector scored as unedited. In both conditions it reported more erased lesions than with the training tools or on the more visible erasures, which is consistent with some sensitivity to unfamiliar edit traces. The decisive comparison is therefore the one with no edit. On unedited polyp-free frames \ms{} reported a polyp on 2 of 103, against 15 for its base model; an answerer that relied on edit traces would have behaved like its base model there.
% src: 04_results_a.tex subsec:res_alternatives (14.6%, 90.4%; unseen tools; low-visibility erasures; residuals); 05_results_b.tex subsec:res_pairs (2 and 15 of 103)

Counterfactual training taught restraint and not detection. On polyp frames \ms{} called no more polyps present than its base model (36 against 40 of 103). In the pre-specified two-by-two comparison, each kind of training image improved the test built from that kind. Counterfactual views raised the ESG on the main test ($0.744$ against $0.260$) and were not shown to add anything on unedited frames ($-0.058$ \ci{-0.168}{0.042}). What the model was trained on moved the result. Changes of the training objective, the adapter, the agent design and the decision rule were not shown to: none of twelve alternative designs and none of 270 decision rules met its pre-specified criterion. Several designs had one seed, so small gains are not excluded. A possible mechanism is that an erased view shows a familiar context without its lesion, which may teach a model not to answer from context, while it adds no new example of a lesion. The pneumothorax result is consistent with this: \ms{} found none of 100 pneumothorax films of new patients, and \msp, with such films in training, found 53.
% src: 05_results_b.tex subsec:res_pairs (36 and 40 of 103) and subsec:res_designs (0.744 against 0.260; -0.058 [-0.168, +0.042]; twelve designs, 270 rules; one seed); 04_results_a.tex subsec:res_external (0 of 100; 53 of 100)

These findings agree with earlier evidence that accuracy does not establish image use, and differ from it in one respect. \citet{jin2024hidden} found by physician reading that $35.5\%$ of the correct answers of one model came with a flawed rationale, most often in image comprehension ($27.2\%$). We found by intervention that correct answers on the original image coexisted with unchanged answers after erasure. In other studies the answers followed the text or survived the loss of the image. Accuracy fell from $84\%$ to $28\%$ for one model when a vignette misled \citep{buckley2026text}. Four models answered $33.7\%$ to $37.7\%$ of image-dependent questions correctly without the image \citep{gu2025illusion}, and four systems kept about half of their correct answers when the radiograph was exchanged for one of the opposite label \citep{lotfinia2026image}. In our study the reports did not survive it: on a blank image every comparison model asked for a better image. The difference may lie in the design, since our questions have no clinical vignette and our protocol allows such a request. The reports we measured arose when an image of the right kind was shown, which removal or exchange of the whole image cannot detect.
% src: papers_g3/jin_npj.md L22, L101 (35.5%, 27.2%); buckley_main.md abstract (84% to 28%); illusion.md L171 (33.7% to 37.7%, four models); lotfinia_main.md abstract (about half of correct answers kept under the opposite-label swap); 04_results_a.tex subsec:res_main (blank-image control)

Erasure with a matched control can establish, at scale, whether an answer depends on what the lesion region shows. It cannot establish that the finding is absent for a specialist: secondary signs outside the mask may remain, and readers were not asked what they saw. Reader support is strongest in colonoscopy and incomplete there. One board-certified gastroenterologist reported the finding on 4 of 17 erased colonoscopy images ($23.5\%$; exact 95\% CI $6.8$ to $49.9$) and on 0 of 7 lesion-free ones. The pre-specified criterion, an upper limit of at most $10\%$, cannot be met by one reader and was not met; the radiologists' readings are not complete. Some erased-lesion claims may therefore be correct readings of residual evidence. The ESG measures dependence on the lesion region, not error against a clinical reference standard.
% src: 04_results_a.tex subsec:res_readers (4 of 17, 23.5%, 6.8 to 49.9; 0 of 7; criterion; 35 of 64 and 23 of 64)

This study has five main limitations. (i) Outside colonoscopy, the reference standard for an erased lesion rests on a reader assessment that is not complete, and trainee readers reported a finding on 35 of 64 reads of erased images (23 of 64 reads of lesion-free images). The specialist's colonoscopy readings and the unedited pairs, which need no erasure, bound this concern for colonoscopy only. (ii) Confirmation on unedited images covers colonoscopy and pneumothorax, and \msn{} was not shown to exceed \gem{} on colonoscopy pairs (lower limit of the difference at the unseen hospital, $-0.017$). On pneumothorax films its Youden index exceeded that of each reference model ($0.660$ against $0.460$, $0.207$ and $0.377$), but the films came from a source used in its training. (iii) \msn{} reported more erased lesions on the fresh external set than \msp, mostly on chest films ($33\%$ against $10\%$), and its pneumothorax detection on new patients varied by seed (54 to 75 of 100). A three-seed vote steadied detection (68 of 100) at three times the computing cost and did not lower those claims ($22.6\%$). (iv) The tests were closed questions on single images from public datasets, without demographic or acquisition data. The open-ended test had 36 cases and did not confirm the advantage, and the comparison models are dated snapshots (Section~\ref{subsec:models}). Across six prompting conditions the three reference models reported $36.8\%$ to $66.2\%$ of erased lesions. (v) \msn{} was designed after earlier results were known, although its tests were fixed before it was trained. The analysis plan is our own dated record, the cost matrix behind net utility was not elicited from clinicians (ESG and the claim rates do not depend on it), and no model was evaluated prospectively.
% src: 04_results_a.tex subsec:res_readers, subsec:res_main (36 cases; 36.8-66.2% on 150 cases); 05_results_b.tex subsec:res_pairs (lower limit -0.017) and subsec:res_nmodel (0.660 against 0.460, 0.207, 0.377; 33% against 10%; 54, 75, 70; vote 68; vote 22.6% in Table tab:nmodel); 03_methods.tex subsec:models (identifiers and access date), subsec:measures (cost matrix), subsec:stats (own dated record)

Three kinds of evidence are needed next. The specialist round has to be completed, so that the erasures can be accepted or rejected for each imaging type and the models compared again on erasures that specialists call clean. Unedited same-patient pairs with a verified reference standard are needed beyond colonoscopy; the chest X-ray follow-up pairs used here were a post hoc secondary analysis and did not separate the models. The consequence for patients of a reported finding that the image does not show can be measured only prospectively, in clinical use.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

On public images and closed questions, nine comparison models reported $41.1\%$ to $61.6\%$ of lesions that had been erased from the image, and none reported the finding on a blank image. A model trained on matched counterfactual views reported $9.6\%$, but this advantage did not carry over to unedited images until unedited images were added to its training. The resulting model exceeded two of three reference models on unedited colonoscopy pairs from an unseen hospital; a difference from the third was not shown, and it reported more erased lesions on a fresh external set. Reader support for the erasures was limited to colonoscopy and incomplete there.
