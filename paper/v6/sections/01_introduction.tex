\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Vision-language models (VLMs) are being proposed as assistants for the interpretation of medical images: a clinician shows an image and asks whether a named finding is present \citep{sellergren2025medgemma,xu2025lingshu,chen2024huatuogpt}. A finding reported on an image that does not show it can lead to further imaging, biopsy or treatment that the patient did not need, and a finding missed on an image that shows it delays diagnosis. A model can avoid either error by making the other, so the two have to be measured together. Neither can be interpreted unless the answer depends on what the image shows.
% src: no numbers

Current evaluations measure accuracy on unaltered images with a reference answer \citep{lau2018vqarad,liu2021slake,he2020pathvqa}. Accuracy on unaltered images has a logical limit: when the image is never varied, a correct answer cannot show that the image was used, because a model that answers from the finding named in the question, or from what usually accompanies the anatomy shown, gives the same answer. Probes that vary the question on an unaltered image show that models affirm findings that are absent \citep{li2023pope,yan2024probmed}, but they leave the same limit in place.
% src: no numbers

Studies that changed the text or the whole image show that this limit matters in practice. On published case challenges, the accuracy of one model fell steeply when a misleading vignette was added \citep{buckley2026text}. On questions chosen because they need the image, most models still answered well above chance without it \citep{gu2025illusion}, and when a chest radiograph was exchanged for one with the opposite label, several systems kept many of their correct answers \citep{lotfinia2026image}. Image classifiers have likewise relied on the hospital of origin or on skin markings rather than on signs of disease \citep{zech2018variable,degrave2021shortcuts,winkler2019markings,oakden2020hidden,geirhos2020shortcut}. Removing or exchanging the whole image, however, changes everything in it at once. It cannot show whether an answer about one finding depends on the visible evidence for that finding.

We set four requirements for an intervention on the finding itself. First, the lesion must be removed while the rest of the image, and the question, stay the same. Second, the act of editing needs a control: an edited image differs from an unedited one in more than the lesion, and inpainting can reveal what was removed through the traces it leaves \citep{rong2022road}, so a model could respond to the edit and not to the absence of the lesion. The control is the same edit applied where there is no lesion. Third, the removal must be checked, because an automatic erasure can leave part of the lesion or its secondary signs, and whether the finding is gone is a judgement for a specialist reader. Fourth, the result must be tested on images with no edit, because models do not meet edited images in use; unedited images of the same patient with and without the finding provide such a test. To our knowledge, no evaluation of medical VLMs has combined an erasure confined to the lesion, a matched control edit and a test on unedited same-patient pairs (Section~\ref{sec:related}).
% src: no numbers

The aim of this study was to test whether the answer of a VLM about a named finding depends on the visible evidence for that finding, and whether training on images that differ in that evidence changes this, on edited and on unedited images. For each case we built an erased image, a control-edited image and an unedited lesion-free image (together, MedScope-Bench). We evaluated nine VLMs accessed through their providers' interfaces (the comparison models) on these images, trained a small open-weight model, \ms, on images built in the same way, and tested it with editing tools not used in training, on external datasets and on unedited same-patient colonoscopy pairs. Physicians who were not told which images were edited read a sample of the erased images. After the result on unedited pairs was known, a second model trained in addition on unedited images, \msn, was designed post hoc and tested on pairs from a hospital not used in training. The images came from public datasets of colonoscopy, chest X-ray, breast ultrasound, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT).
