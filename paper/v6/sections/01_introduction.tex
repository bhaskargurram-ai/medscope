\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Vision-language models (VLMs) are being proposed as assistants for the interpretation of medical images: a clinician shows an image and asks whether a named finding is present \citep{sellergren2025medgemma,xu2025lingshu,chen2024huatuogpt}. Two errors matter in this setting. A finding reported on an image that does not show it can lead to further imaging, biopsy or treatment that the patient did not need. A finding missed on an image that shows it delays diagnosis. A model can avoid either error by making the other, so the two have to be measured together. Both depend on whether the answer follows what the image shows.
% src: no numbers

Current evaluations measure accuracy on unaltered images with a reference answer \citep{lau2018vqarad,liu2021slake,he2020pathvqa}. This has a logical limit. When the image is never varied, a correct answer cannot show that the image was used: a model that answers from the finding named in the question, or from what usually accompanies the anatomy shown, gives the same answer. Probes that vary the question on an unaltered image show that models affirm findings that are absent \citep{li2023pope,yan2024probmed}, but they leave the same limit in place. Interventions on the text or on the whole image show that it matters. On published case challenges, accuracy rose with the amount of informative text, and one model fell from 84\% to 28\% when a misleading vignette was added \citep{buckley2026text}. On 175 questions chosen because they need the image, four of five models still answered 33.7\% to 37.7\% correctly without it, against 20\% by chance \citep{gu2025illusion}. When a chest radiograph was exchanged for one with the opposite label, four systems kept about half of their correct answers \citep{lotfinia2026image}. Image classifiers have likewise used the hospital of origin or skin markings in place of the disease \citep{zech2018variable,degrave2021shortcuts,winkler2019markings,oakden2020hidden,geirhos2020shortcut}. Removing or exchanging the whole image, however, changes everything in it at once. It cannot show whether an answer about one finding depends on the visible evidence for that finding.

An intervention on the finding itself has four requirements. First, the lesion must be removed while the rest of the image, and the question, stay the same. Second, the act of editing needs a control. An edited image differs from an unedited one in more than the lesion, and removal by inpainting can reveal what was removed through the traces it leaves \citep{rong2022road}. A model could therefore respond to the edit and not to the absence of the lesion. The control is the same edit applied where there is no lesion. Third, the removal must be checked, because an automatic erasure can leave part of the lesion or its secondary signs, and only a specialist reader can say whether the finding is gone. Fourth, the result must be tested on images with no edit, because an edited image is not one that a model meets in use. Unedited images of the same patient with and without the finding provide such a test. To our knowledge, no evaluation of medical VLMs has combined these four elements (Section~\ref{sec:related}).
% src: no numbers

The aim of this study was to test whether the answer of a VLM about a named finding depends on the visible evidence for that finding, and whether training on images that differ in that evidence makes it do so. We built matched erased, control-edited and lesion-free views (MedScope-Bench), evaluated nine frontier models on them, trained an open-weight model on such views, tested it with unseen editing tools and on unedited same-patient pairs, and assessed the erasures with specialist readers. A second model, trained in addition on unedited images, was tested on pairs from a hospital not used in training. The images came from public datasets of colonoscopy, chest radiography, breast ultrasound, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT).
